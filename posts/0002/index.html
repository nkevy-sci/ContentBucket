Probability & Basketball

An Example

This is a fun statistics example that finds the probability of one basketball player scoring more points than another. It was inspired by Hooke (1983), as cited by Stark (2023).

Two basketball players’ points per game are described in the following table.

Table 1: Points across six games
Player Game 1 Game 2 Game 3 Game 4 Game 5 Game 6
Melanie 19 17 19 10 17 17
Kim 20 10 10 13 19 10

Independence

Melanie and Kim play on different teams. In fact, their teams have never played against each other. So, we’ll assume their points per game are independent, meaning Melanie’s performance does not affect Kim’s, and Kim’s performance does not affect Melanie’s.

Probability

The performance of each of these players, Melanie and Kim, can be described in terms of chance or probability. In other words, these data allow you to make an educated guess about their future performance.

What are the chances that Melanie scores more points than Kim in the next game?

It’s a good question, and the first step is to get clear about what’s possible. Because there are data for only six games, you really should not assume that Melanie’s scores can be modeled by well-known pattern such as the normal distribution. Melanie could even score 0 points next game, who knows.

In statistics, making fewer assumptions about the underlying distribution of a dataset is called a nonparametric approach. Basically, the term means that you're trying to make less assumptions about the possible scores (credit: Hoskin, n.d.).

The second step is to define the question and search for an appropriate statistical test. You want to know who will score more points. If Melanie and Kim played at exactly the same level, there is a 50% probability that Melanie will either score more points than Kim or tie. To keep things simple, you count ties as a half win. These ideas become the following null hypothesis.

H0:P(Melanie>Kim)+12⁢P(Melanie=Kim)=12

On the other hand, if Melanie is a better shooter than Kim, the probability is different; it won’t be a 50% chance. Melanie will tend to score more points. This is the alternative hypothesis.

H1:P(Melanie>Kim)+12⁢P(Melanie=Kim)>12

The Mann–Whitney U Test

A quick online search shows that the Mann–Whitney U test will help you figure this all out (Shier, 2004). Simply put, the Mann–Whitney U test compares every game that Melanie played against every game that Kim played. Take a look at their games again.

Table 4: Points across six games
Player Game 1 Game 2 Game 3 Game 4 Game 5 Game 6
Melanie 19 17 19 10 17 17
Kim 20 10 10 13 19 10

For each of Melanie’s games, count how many times her score is greater than Kim’s. If it’s a tie, count it as 0.5 instead of 1. Call this value uM.

Table 5: Test statistics for Melanie’s scores
Player Score 1 Score 2 Score 3 Score 4 Score 5 Score 6
Melanie 19 17 19 10 17 17
uM 4.5— 4— 4.5— 1.5— 4— 4—

For example, 19 was less than 20, so you didn’t count that one. Yet 19 is better than 10, so you do count this one. Then 19 is better than 10 and 13, equal to 19, and greater than 10. So, in total, that’s 4.5. In Table 5 above, you’d write 4.5 under 19. Afterwards, sum along the row to get the U statistic.

Table 6: Comparing test statistics
Player U
Melanie 22.5
Kim 13.5
Total 36

These U values are called Mann-Whitney U test statistics. For this example, you’ll select Melanie's U because it represents her scoring more than Kim. Basically, the test is a comparison of this U statistic to another number, called the critical value.

If Melanie's U passes the test, there is some evidence supporting your hypothesis. In formal terms, a passing test means there is some evidence against the null hypothesis. In basic terms, it means that the event you observed had a very small probability (say 5% or less) of happening by random chance.

For this example, you can use a lookup table to approximate the critical value. This table requires you to use 22.5, the larger U statistic. It passes the test if U is greater than or equal to the critical value on page 3 where the sixth row and sixth column intersect. Click to view the lookup table (Laemmerzahl, n.d.).

Or! You can calculate the critical value, but it requires a computer. Simply put, you have to find all the possible U statistics, sort them, and count repeats. Afterwards, you'd see that the critical value is 28.

Unfortunately, because the critical value is 28 (or 29) and that's greater than 22.5. So the test fails.

22.5Melanie<28Critical

An Educated Guess

Well, there’s some bad news. There isn’t strong evidence that Melanie is better than Kim. In formal terms, this test has failed to reject the null hypothesis at the 5% significance level.

But! There is good news too. You can still make an educated guess. If the Mann–Whitney U test fails, you can calculate the Common Language Effect Size (CLES) instead. While there's a lots of uncertainty, it is still evidence-based reasoning; and at this point, it’s easy to calculate.

22.5Melanie36=.625

You simply divide Melanie’s U statistic by the total number of comparisons. In plain English, this is the probability that Melanie’s score will be greater than Kim’s if you randomly pick one score for each player and adjust for ties. So, best guess: Melanie has a 62.5% chance of scoring more points than Kim during the next game.
